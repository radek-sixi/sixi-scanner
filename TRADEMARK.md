## Why this document exists

Apache License 2.0 grants copyright and patent rights and stops there. Section 6
says so explicitly:

> "Trademarks. This License does not grant permission to use the trade names,
> trademarks, service marks, or product names of the Licensor, except as required
> for reasonable and customary use in describing the origin of the Work."

So anyone may take this code, use it commercially, and change every line of it.
What they may not do is present their version as the one I published, or ship it
under my name. This document is where that boundary is written down, because a
boundary nobody can find is not a boundary.

## What you may do

**Nominative use.** Saying what you use is fine and expected. None of these need
permission:

- "We use Sixi Scanner to red-team our agents."
- "Our red-teaming pipeline runs Sixi Scanner, among other tools."
- Linking to this repository from your own documentation, blog post, talk or CV.
- Naming it in a security assessment, an audit response, or a procurement answer,
  where naming the actual tool is what makes the statement useful.
- Distributing or selling an unmodified copy, including in a product or appliance,
  provided the Apache 2.0 notices travel with it as section 4(d) requires.
- Forking it under a name of your own. The code is yours to fork. See below.

## What you may not do without permission

**Shipping a modified version under my name.** If you add a technique, change a
marker, or wire it into a product, it is no longer Sixi Scanner and cannot be
distributed as one. Fork it, rename it, and be explicit that it is yours.

**Implying endorsement or affiliation.** Do not use the mark in your company
name, product name, domain name or package name. Do not write "based on Sixi
Scanner", "powered by Sixi Scanner", or "compatible with Sixi Scanner" unless
that is literally true and you have asked.

**Naming a commercial offer after it.** A hosted service, a consultancy product
or a support plan called "Sixi Scanner" or "Sixi Scanner Enterprise" needs
permission, whether or not the code underneath is Apache 2.0. This is the case
the policy exists for, and it is the one I will act on.

**Implying that I will maintain your fork.** The name carries an implicit
promise of support. Taking it means telling people something untrue about who
will answer their issue.

## What permission looks like

Open an issue asking, and say what you want to do. I answer these — there is no
automated trademark bot and no legal review queue.

I will usually say yes to a fork, a course, a conference talk, a book, or a
research paper. I will usually say no to a commercial offer under the name,
because that is the thing that makes the name worth protecting.

## Honest limitations

This is a usage policy, not a registered trade mark, and it is worth being clear
about what that means.

- **I have not registered this as a trade mark.** Rights here come from
  established use and reputation, which the policy supports but does not create.
- **Rights accrue from use, so publishing matters.** A mark nobody has seen
  cannot be protected. Every public use of the name — this repository, the
  release, the documentation — is what builds the right to say no to someone
  later. Use it consistently and don't quietly rebrand it.
- **A clause is not enforcement.** If someone ignores this, the practical
  remedies are a takedown, a letter, or a DMCA-style claim on the Apache notices.
  The notice is in `NOTICE` precisely so that removing it leaves a trail.
- **I will not litigate over this.** The realistic worst case I will act on is
  someone selling a product under the name. Everything below that threshold, I
  will settle with a message.

If you redistribute this code, keep `NOTICE`. If you want to be explicit about
provenance in your own docs, the honest and complete form is:

Attribution is a licence obligation and a courtesy. The courtesy costs nothing
and the obligation is already covered by Apache 2.0 section 4(d); you do not need
permission to be accurate about where the code came from.
